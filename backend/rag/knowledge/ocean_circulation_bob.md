# Bay of Bengal Circulation & Dynamics

## East India Coastal Current (EICC)
The EICC is a seasonally reversing boundary current along India's eastern coast. During the southwest monsoon (June-September) it flows northward, while during the northeast monsoon (November-February) it reverses to flow southward. This current plays a crucial role in redistributing freshwater from river discharge.

## Sri Lanka Dome
The Sri Lanka Dome is a semi-permanent cyclonic eddy located southwest of Sri Lanka, centred near 8°N, 83°E. It forms due to wind stress curl during the southwest monsoon and is associated with upwelling of cold, nutrient-rich water from depth.

## River Plume Dynamics
The Ganges-Brahmaputra river system discharges approximately 1,200 km³ of freshwater annually into the northern Bay of Bengal, peaking during the southwest monsoon (July-September). This creates an extremely fresh surface layer (salinity below 28 PSU) that spreads southward along the coast and eastward into the central bay.

## Mesoscale Eddies
The Bay of Bengal is rich in mesoscale eddies (50-200 km diameter) that form from instabilities in the boundary currents and wind forcing. These eddies transport heat, salt, and nutrients, and they influence mixed layer depth and tropical cyclone intensification.

## Barrier Layer
When the halocline is shallower than the isothermal layer depth, a barrier layer forms. This is common in the Bay of Bengal due to heavy freshwater input. The barrier layer traps warm water near the surface, inhibiting vertical mixing and maintaining high sea surface temperatures that can fuel tropical cyclones.

## Andaman Sea
The Andaman Sea, east of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, connects to the Bay of Bengal through several channels. It has its own internal tides and circulation patterns, with relatively higher salinity than the central bay due to reduced freshwater input.
