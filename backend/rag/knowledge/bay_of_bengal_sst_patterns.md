# Bay of Bengal Sea Surface Temperature (SST) Patterns

## Overview
The Bay of Bengal (BoB) exhibits distinct seasonal Sea Surface Temperature (SST) patterns driven primarily by the Indian monsoon system and solar radiation cycles.

## Seasonal SST Patterns
- **Pre-monsoon (March–May)**: SST reaches its annual peak, often exceeding 30°C in the northern BoB. This warm surface layer is critical for tropical cyclone genesis.
- **Southwest Monsoon (June–September)**: SST drops slightly (28–30°C) due to enhanced wind-driven mixing, freshwater influx from rivers (Ganges, Brahmaputra), and reduced solar insolation.
- **Post-monsoon (October–December)**: SST remains warm (27–29°C), supporting continued cyclone activity. The Northeast Monsoon brings SST cooling in December.
- **Winter (January–February)**: Coolest SST of the year (24–26°C), driven by reduced solar heating and increased wind mixing.

## Bay of Bengal SST Characteristics
- **Barrier Layer**: The BoB is unique in having a "barrier layer" between the mixed layer base and the thermocline top, caused by intense freshwater river runoff (Ganges, Brahmaputra) and monsoonal precipitation that stabilizes the salinity-driven density gradient and makes the Bay of Bengal fresher at the surface. This prevents cool thermocline water from reaching the surface even during storms.
- **Warm Pool**: The northern BoB acts as a persistent warm pool (>28°C) for most of the year, feeding latent heat to monsoon convection.
- **Spatial Gradients**: SST is typically warmer in the north (freshwater stratified) and slightly cooler near the Sri Lanka coast (coastal upwelling).

## ARGO Float SST Observations
ARGO floats measure temperature at 5–10 dbar pressure (near-surface level). The Bay of Bengal ARGO array captures:
- Seasonal SST warming and cooling cycles
- Subsurface thermal structure below the warm surface layer
- Pre- and post-cyclone temperature changes (cooling of 1–3°C)

## Impact on Oceanographic Research
SST from ARGO floats is used to validate satellite infrared SST products (e.g., MODIS, AVHRR) and to study ocean-atmosphere heat exchange during monsoon onset.
